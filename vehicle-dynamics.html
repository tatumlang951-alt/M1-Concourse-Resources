Understand the car

Introduction to vehicle dynamics

A first-order setup guide: connect common adjustments to what the driver feels, then test those ideas against data.

How to use this guideThese are generalized first-order tendencies, not universal rules. Results depend on the vehicle, tire, track, aero map, geometry, driver inputs, and other setup parameters. Change one clearly defined thing, record conditions, and compare driver feedback with measurements.

Aerodynamics

More front aero

Typical effect
More front lateral capacity at speed; usually less high-speed understeer.
Where it matters
Fast corners
Why
More aerodynamic load at the front shifts the available axle force balance.
Opposite adjustment
Less front aero usually shifts balance toward high-speed understeer.
Conditions
Tire load sensitivity, drag, and the complete aero map still matter.

More rear aero

Typical effect
Usually more rear stability and less high-speed oversteer.
Where it matters
Fast corners and exit
Why
Additional rear vertical load increases rear lateral capacity.
Opposite adjustment
Less rear aero can reduce rear stability while reducing drag.
Conditions
The balance shift matters as well as the total downforce.

Aero balance forward / rearward

Typical effect
Forward generally adds high-speed rotation; rearward generally adds stability.
Where it matters
Fast corners
Why
The front share of aerodynamic load changes relative to the rear.
Opposite adjustment
Moving balance rearward reverses the usual tendency of moving it forward.
Conditions
A balance change does not necessarily mean more total downforce.

Lower front ride height / splitter

Typical effect
Can increase front or total downforce within the intended operating window.
Where it matters
High-speed running
Why
Changes the pressure distribution around the nose and underfloor.
Opposite adjustment
Raising the front changes ground interaction and may reduce load in that operating range.
Conditions
Too low can cause bottoming, flow separation, choking, or abrupt aero changes. Use the aero map.

Lower rear ride height

Typical effect
Changes floor and diffuser performance and aero balance.
Where it matters
High-speed running
Why
Changes underfloor geometry, expansion, and pressure recovery.
Opposite adjustment
Raising the rear moves the vehicle to a different aero-map operating point.
Conditions
There is no universal understeer or oversteer direction.

More rake

Typical effect
Changes downforce and aero balance.
Where it matters
Aero-platform control
Why
Changes the inclination of the vehicle and underfloor relative to the track.
Opposite adjustment
Less rake creates a flatter platform and a different aero operating point.
Conditions
More rake does not always add front grip. Evaluate ride height, yaw, roll, and pitch together.

More front wing angle

Typical effect
Usually more front load and drag before stall.
Where it matters
Fast corners and straights
Why
Changes wing circulation and pressure difference.
Opposite adjustment
Less angle generally reduces front load and drag.
Conditions
Effects depend on geometry, interaction with the rest of the car, and flow separation.

More rear wing angle

Typical effect
Usually more rear grip and stability, with more drag.
Where it matters
Fast corners and straights
Why
Increases rear aerodynamic loading within the useful range.
Opposite adjustment
Less angle usually reduces rear load and can improve straight-line speed.
Conditions
Maximum speed also depends on power, gearing, and the rest of the aero package.

Larger gurney / wicker

Typical effect
Can increase element loading and drag.
Where it matters
High-speed running
Why
Changes the trailing-edge flow and pressure distribution.
Opposite adjustment
A smaller gurney generally reduces its loading effect.
Conditions
The location determines which axle gains load; gains are not unlimited.

More effective diffuser

Typical effect
Can increase underfloor contribution to downforce.
Where it matters
High-speed running
Why
A well-matched diffuser supports useful underbody flow and pressure recovery.
Opposite adjustment
Reduced diffuser effectiveness can reduce underfloor load.
Conditions
Handling follows the resulting aero balance. “More diffuser” does not automatically mean more rotation.

Drag trim

Typical effect
Can improve straight-line speed while reducing aero grip.
Where it matters
Straights versus fast corners
Why
Trades aerodynamic load for lower resistance.
Opposite adjustment
More downforce generally brings greater drag and cornering potential.
Conditions
Choose for the circuit, passing requirements, and vehicle power.

Springs & anti-roll bars

Stiffer front springs

Typical effect
More platform support; often an understeer tendency and less compliance.
Where it matters
Braking, roll, and sustained cornering
Why
Raises front wheel/ride stiffness and can increase the front share of elastic lateral load transfer.
Opposite adjustment
Softer front springs allow more movement and may improve mechanical grip on uneven surfaces.
Conditions
Aero-platform benefits, geometry, travel, and bump-stop contact can outweigh the simple mechanical tendency.

Stiffer rear springs

Typical effect
More rear platform support; often more rotation and less rear compliance.
Where it matters
Cornering and throttle transitions
Why
Can increase the rear share of elastic lateral load transfer and reduce surface compliance.
Opposite adjustment
Softer rear springs often improve rear compliance but allow more platform movement.
Conditions
Evaluate the actual wheel rates and aero-platform response, not just the spring labels.

Stiffer front anti-roll bar

Typical effect
Usually more understeer.
Where it matters
Lateral loading
Why
Increases the front share of elastic lateral load transfer, which can reduce combined front-tire capability through load sensitivity.
Opposite adjustment
A softer front bar usually reduces that front share and can improve front mechanical grip.
Conditions
Camber control, suspension geometry, inside-wheel unloading, and aero can alter the outcome.

Stiffer rear anti-roll bar

Typical effect
Usually more rotation or oversteer tendency.
Where it matters
Lateral loading
Why
Increases the rear share of elastic lateral load transfer.
Opposite adjustment
A softer rear bar usually adds rear grip or stability.
Conditions
An unloaded inside driven tire can limit traction; differential behavior matters.

Stiffer at both ends

Typical effect
Less movement and potentially better platform control, not automatically more grip.
Where it matters
Roll, pitch, and heave
Why
Changes body-motion response and compliance; stiffness distribution determines much of the balance effect.
Opposite adjustment
Softer overall settings increase movement and surface compliance.
Conditions
Preserve adequate travel and evaluate tires, track roughness, and aero together.

Earlier bump-stop / bump-spring engagement

Typical effect
Can sharply increase effective wheel rate and change balance.
Where it matters
Large displacement and aero loading
Why
An additional spring element becomes active as suspension travel is used.
Opposite adjustment
Later engagement preserves the initial rate over more travel.
Conditions
Rate, gap, motion ratio, and which corners engage are all important.

Dampers

Dampers primarily shape transient motion and the timing of load transfer. They do not simply change the total acceleration-driven load transfer.

Damper shaft-velocity guide

“Low speed” and “high speed” describe how fast the damper shaft moves, not how fast the car travels. The dividing point depends on the damper and its valving.

Low shaft velocity

Often associated with roll, pitch, heave, brake application/release, and steering transitions.

High shaft velocity

Often associated with curbs, sharp bumps, surface impacts, and sudden wheel movement.

Compression: the damper shortens. Rebound: the damper extends. Adjuster ranges can overlap, so use the damper’s force–velocity curves and documentation.

More front low-speed compression

Typical effect
Resists front compression and changes pitch/platform response.
Where it matters
Brake application and turn-in
Why
The damper produces more opposing force while the suspension compresses in the relevant velocity range.
Opposite adjustment
Less compression damping allows faster compression and can improve compliance.
Conditions
Too much can transmit disturbances into the chassis; do not assume a universal balance change.

More rear low-speed compression

Typical effect
Resists rear compression and changes squat/roll response.
Where it matters
Throttle application and transitions
Why
Opposes rear suspension compression while it is moving.
Opposite adjustment
Less damping permits faster rear compression.
Conditions
Traction and rotation depend on geometry, tires, and the transient being considered.

More front low-speed rebound

Typical effect
Slows front extension and can keep the front lower for longer during a transition.
Where it matters
Brake release and acceleration transitions
Why
Rebound force opposes suspension extension.
Opposite adjustment
Less rebound allows faster extension.
Conditions
It does not universally cause entry understeer; consider the complete maneuver.

More rear low-speed rebound

Typical effect
Slows rear extension and changes transient rear loading.
Where it matters
Braking and roll transitions
Why
Resists rear droop while the suspension extends.
Opposite adjustment
Less rebound permits faster extension.
Conditions
The handling direction depends on which tires are extending and the vehicle architecture.

More high-speed compression

Typical effect
Greater resistance to rapid compression; potentially less bump compliance.
Where it matters
Curbs, sharp bumps, and impacts
Why
Produces more force during rapid shaft movement.
Opposite adjustment
Less high-speed compression can let the wheel absorb an impact more readily.
Conditions
Too little can permit excess travel or bottoming; too much can reduce contact quality.

More high-speed rebound

Typical effect
Slows wheel extension after a rapid compression event.
Where it matters
Recovery after bumps and curbs
Why
Opposes rapid suspension extension.
Opposite adjustment
Less rebound lets the wheel return more quickly but can allow oscillation.
Conditions
Excessive rebound can prevent recovery between repeated bumps and contribute to packing.

Alignment & steering

More front toe-out

Typical effect
Often sharper initial response, with more scrub and potentially less straight-line stability.
Where it matters
Initial turn-in and straight-line running
Why
Changes the front tires’ initial directions and slip behavior.
Opposite adjustment
More toe-in often favors straight-line stability with a different initial response.
Conditions
Temperature, wear, compliance steer, and series conventions matter.

More rear toe-in

Typical effect
Usually more rear stability and less willingness to rotate.
Where it matters
Corner entry and exit
Why
Changes rear tire slip behavior and stabilizing response.
Opposite adjustment
Less toe-in can free the rear; toe-out can be aggressive and destabilizing.
Conditions
More toe also increases scrub and can add heat and drag.

More negative front camber

Typical effect
Can increase lateral grip toward the tire’s preferred loaded camber.
Where it matters
Cornering
Why
Helps the loaded tire operate at a favorable contact-patch orientation.
Opposite adjustment
Less negative camber may improve the straight-line footprint but reduce cornering capability.
Conditions
Beyond optimum it can reduce grip and increase inside-edge wear; braking is a tradeoff.

More negative rear camber

Typical effect
Can increase rear lateral grip toward optimum.
Where it matters
Cornering versus acceleration
Why
Changes the orientation and load distribution of the rear contact patch.
Opposite adjustment
Less negative camber may support longitudinal traction at the cost of lateral performance.
Conditions
Excessive camber can hurt drive and braking capability.

More positive caster

Typical effect
Changes steering-induced camber, self-centering, steering effort, and jacking.
Where it matters
Steering and cornering
Why
Alters the steering-axis geometry and wheel motion as steer angle changes.
Opposite adjustment
Less caster reduces some of those geometric effects.
Conditions
Do not equate more caster with a guaranteed gain in grip or driver feel.

More Ackermann

Typical effect
Changes the inner-to-outer front steering-angle relationship.
Where it matters
Low-speed turns and loaded cornering
Why
Inside and outside tires follow different radii and may require different slip angles.
Opposite adjustment
Reduced or anti-Ackermann changes that relationship in the other direction.
Conditions
Race-tire load and slip-angle requirements may favor reduced Ackermann; more is not always better.

Faster steering ratio

Typical effect
More road-wheel angle for the same steering-wheel movement.
Where it matters
Driver inputs
Why
Changes the conversion between steering-wheel and road-wheel angle.
Opposite adjustment
A slower ratio requires more hand movement for the same wheel angle.
Conditions
It does not inherently create more tire grip.

Track width, mass & geometry

Wider front track

Typical effect
Often reduces front load transfer for a given front roll moment and may improve axle grip.
Where it matters
Lateral loading
Why
A wider lateral lever arm can support the same roll moment with a smaller wheel-load difference.
Opposite adjustment
Narrowing front track tends to increase that load difference, all else equal.
Conditions
Geometry, roll stiffness, camber, and overall load-transfer distribution also change.

Wider rear track

Typical effect
Can improve rear axle grip or stability under comparable conditions.
Where it matters
Lateral loading
Why
Changes the lever arm that reacts rear roll moment.
Opposite adjustment
A narrower rear track can increase load difference and change rotation.
Conditions
Kart behavior is particularly coupled to jacking and inside-rear unloading; do not transfer car rules blindly.

Wheel offset change

Typical effect
Changes track width and may change scrub radius and steering loads.
Where it matters
Steering, bearings, and suspension loading
Why
Moves the contact patch relative to the steering axis and hub.
Opposite adjustment
The reverse offset change moves those lever arms back the other way.
Conditions
Specify the actual wheel/contact-patch position. “More offset” alone is an ambiguous handling instruction.

Lower center of gravity

Typical effect
Generally reduces acceleration-driven lateral and longitudinal load transfer.
Where it matters
Braking, acceleration, and cornering
Why
Reduces the moment arm between the center of gravity and the ground.
Opposite adjustment
A higher CG increases those moments.
Conditions
Ground clearance, geometry, and aero-platform limits still apply.

Move weight forward

Typical effect
Increases front static load and often shifts steady-state balance toward understeer.
Where it matters
Steady cornering and braking
Why
Changes axle loads, tire load sensitivity, and the force each axle must provide.
Opposite adjustment
Moving weight rearward increases rear static load and may help rear-drive traction.
Conditions
Brake balance, tires, wheelbase, and transient behavior matter; entry stability is not guaranteed.

Centralize mass

Typical effect
Reduces yaw inertia and can make yaw response quicker.
Where it matters
Transient direction changes
Why
Placing mass closer to the CG reduces its contribution to rotational inertia.
Opposite adjustment
Moving mass farther from the CG increases yaw inertia.
Conditions
Faster response is not automatically easier to drive.

Oval setup: crossweight / wedge, rounds & stagger

Oval / generalized. Conventions and direction of travel matter. Wedge commonly refers to crossweight or an adjustment to it. Rounds are turns of a spring perch or jack bolt used to change the setup. They can change wedge and ride height, but rounds and wedge are not the same measurement.

Crossweight % = (RF + LR) / total vehicle weight × 100
Stagger = outside tire circumference − inside tire circumference

More RF/LR crossweight (wedge)

Typical effect
Often a tighter tendency or more on-throttle stability in a conventional left-turn oval setup.
Where it matters
Oval cornering and exit
Why
Changes the diagonal distribution of static tire loads.
Opposite adjustment
Less RF/LR crossweight often frees the car.
Conditions
Highly dependent on banking, geometry, springs, stagger, track direction, and rear suspension.

Rounds: spring-perch / jack-bolt adjustment

Typical effect
Changes corner loads, diagonal loads, and often ride height.
Where it matters
Static setup and platform
Why
Changes spring installation or preload and the equilibrium of the connected vehicle.
Opposite adjustment
Lowering it changes the equilibrium in the opposite adjustment direction.
Conditions
A “round” is a hardware adjustment, not a fixed number of pounds. Verify on scales and with ride-height measurements.

Change both front perches

Typical effect
Changes front ride height and can change rake and aero balance.
Where it matters
Static platform and high-speed running
Why
Moves the vehicle’s operating point and may affect suspension travel.
Opposite adjustment
The opposite change moves front platform height the other way.
Conditions
Recheck all corner loads and ride heights rather than assuming they remain unchanged.

Change both rear perches

Typical effect
Changes rear ride height, rake, and floor/diffuser operating point.
Where it matters
Static platform and high-speed running
Why
Alters the rear platform and suspension operating position.
Opposite adjustment
The opposite adjustment changes rear platform height in the other direction.
Conditions
Use scales, measured heights, and the aero map together.

More rear stagger

Typical effect
Can increase the natural turning tendency in the intended direction.
Where it matters
Oval and solid-axle kart applications
Why
Different rolling circumferences influence the path the coupled rear wheels tend to follow.
Opposite adjustment
Less stagger reduces that natural turning tendency.
Conditions
Direction, tire growth, slip, loading, and differential architecture matter. Cold and hot stagger can differ.

Geometric rear steer

Typical effect
Can change yaw response and cornering attitude.
Where it matters
Suspension movement and cornering
Why
Rear-wheel direction changes the force direction and desired vehicle path.
Opposite adjustment
Reversing the steer direction changes its influence on yaw.
Conditions
“Toward the corner” is not a reliable universal rotation rule: coordinate convention, phase, and geometry must be specified.

Aerodynamic skew / yaw

Typical effect
Changes downforce, sideforce, drag, and aero balance.
Where it matters
Speed and cornering attitude
Why
Air meets the body and aero surfaces at a different yaw angle.
Opposite adjustment
Opposite yaw or less skew produces another point on the aero map.
Conditions
Do not assume more skew always makes the car loose.

Brakes

More front brake bias

Typical effect
More front braking contribution and often better rear stability under braking.
Where it matters
Braking and entry
Why
Moves a greater share of braking torque to the front axle.
Opposite adjustment
More rear bias may add entry rotation but raises rear-lock risk.
Conditions
Too much front bias can lock the fronts early and reduce maximum deceleration.

More duct blanking / brake tape

Typical effect
Raises brake temperatures and may reduce cooling drag.
Where it matters
Repeated braking
Why
Reduces cooling airflow through the brake system.
Opposite adjustment
Opening ducts increases cooling and can lower component temperatures.
Conditions
Too hot can cause fade, fluid overheating, wear, or thermal damage; too cold can also be outside the friction material’s window.

Larger / heavier / thicker rotor

Typical effect
More thermal capacity and generally slower temperature rise for a given energy input.
Where it matters
High brake-energy conditions
Why
More thermal mass can absorb more energy for a given temperature increase.
Opposite adjustment
A smaller or lighter rotor reduces mass but has less thermal reserve.
Conditions
Diameter also changes torque leverage. Added rotating and unsprung mass, packaging, and cooling are tradeoffs.

More rotor ventilation

Typical effect
Can improve heat rejection and change the thermal response.
Where it matters
Sustained braking
Why
Moves more cooling air through the disc when the ducting and rotation are suitable.
Opposite adjustment
Less ventilation reduces cooling potential under comparable conditions.
Conditions
Rotor design and duct routing matter; more airflow is not always the target.

Iron versus carbon systems

Typical effect
Different friction behavior, thermal properties, and useful temperature ranges.
Where it matters
Series-specific braking conditions
Why
Materials behave differently under heat, pressure, wear, and environmental conditions.
Opposite adjustment
Changing material requires a compatible pad/disc system and operating approach.
Conditions
Neither is universally superior. Follow the system manufacturer’s guidance.

Higher-friction front / rear pad

Typical effect
Can shift effective braking-torque distribution toward the axle with the higher friction coefficient.
Where it matters
Braking
Why
Pad friction contributes to brake torque along with clamp force and effective radius.
Opposite adjustment
Lower friction on that axle can shift the effective distribution away from it.
Conditions
Coefficient varies with temperature, pressure, and speed; do not treat compounds simply as “hard” or “soft.”

More initial bite / flatter friction-temperature curve

Typical effect
Higher bite feels more immediate; a flatter curve can give more consistent response through temperature changes.
Where it matters
Brake application and changing thermal conditions
Why
Pad compounds differ in friction, release, modulation, and temperature response.
Opposite adjustment
Less bite may feel less aggressive; a changing friction curve can make response vary more.
Conditions
Consider fade, pad wear, disc wear, and modulation as well as peak friction.

Differential

More power-side lock

Typical effect
More rear-wheel coupling under throttle; can add stability or power understeer.
Where it matters
Acceleration and exit
Why
Resists speed difference between the driven wheels under power.
Opposite adjustment
Less power lock permits more differentiation but may increase inside-wheel spin.
Conditions
Architecture, tire loading, torque, and whether one or both tires saturate can change the handling direction.

More coast-side lock

Typical effect
Often greater entry stability.
Where it matters
Off-throttle and braking phases
Why
Increases wheel coupling in the coast operating condition.
Opposite adjustment
Less coast lock often permits more entry rotation.
Conditions
Effect depends on the differential, brake distribution, and tire loading.

More preload

Typical effect
More resistance to differentiation around low-torque or transition conditions.
Where it matters
Neutral torque and power/coast transitions
Why
Adds an initial resisting torque before some torque-sensitive mechanisms dominate.
Opposite adjustment
Less preload allows freer differentiation near neutral conditions.
Conditions
Terminology and adjustment response differ by differential architecture.

Tire pressures

Think in terms of an operating window, not “higher pressure = less grip.”

Pressure below the useful window

Typical effect
Can cause excess carcass movement, poor support, heat, or sluggish response.
Where it matters
The full run
Why
The tire is not adequately supported for its construction and load.
Opposite adjustment
Raising pressure toward the target can improve response, footprint behavior, and grip.
Conditions
Use the tire manufacturer’s limits and measured behavior; low pressure is not automatically more grip.

Pressure above the useful window

Typical effect
Can reduce compliance and change the footprint unfavorably.
Where it matters
The full run
Why
Higher pressure changes tire stiffness, deformation, and load distribution.
Opposite adjustment
Reducing pressure toward the target may recover compliance and performance.
Conditions
Pressure is not a monotonic grip adjustment: both too low and too high can be poor.

Choose cold pressure for the hot target

Typical effect
A suitable starting pressure helps reach the desired operating window.
Where it matters
Warm-up and sustained running
Why
Pressure changes as the tire heats and its gas temperature changes.
Opposite adjustment
A different cold setting produces a different hot result only under comparable conditions.
Conditions
Track temperature, loading, driving, brake heat, construction, starting temperature, and run length all affect growth.

Terms to learn next

Slip angle
The angle between the direction a tire points and the direction its contact patch travels.
Slip ratio
A measure of the difference between circumferential tire speed and road speed during driving or braking.
Tire load sensitivity
The tendency for tire force capacity to increase less than proportionally as vertical load increases.
Friction circle / ellipse
A simplified picture of how a tire shares its available force between braking, driving, and cornering.
Lateral load transfer
The change in left/right tire loads associated with cornering.
Longitudinal load transfer
The change in front/rear tire loads associated with braking or acceleration.
Roll center
A geometric reference used to describe suspension force transmission and body roll.
Roll couple
The rolling moment associated with lateral forces and their lever arms.
Wheel rate
The effective spring stiffness measured at the wheel.
Motion ratio
The relationship between wheel movement and spring or damper movement; always state the convention.
Ride rate
The effective vertical stiffness seen by the sprung mass, including relevant compliances such as the tire.
Natural frequency
The frequency at which a system tends to oscillate when disturbed.
Critical damping
The damping level separating oscillatory from non-oscillatory response in an ideal second-order system.
Aero balance
The front/rear distribution of aerodynamic load.
Center of pressure
The point through which the resultant aerodynamic force is represented as acting.
Understeer gradient
A measure of how required steering changes with lateral acceleration under specified steady-state conditions.
Differential locking
Resistance to a speed difference between the driven wheels.
Crossweight
The share of total static vehicle weight carried by a specified diagonal pair.
Stagger
The difference between outside and inside tire rolling circumferences.
Bump steer
A change in wheel steer angle as the suspension moves.
Anti-dive
Suspension geometry that reacts part of the braking pitch moment through suspension forces.
Anti-squat
Suspension geometry that reacts part of the acceleration pitch moment through suspension forces.
